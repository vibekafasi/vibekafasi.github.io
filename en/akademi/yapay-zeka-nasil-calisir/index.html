How does AI work? (language model, tokens, prediction)

What is the language model behind Claude and ChatGPT, what are tokens, and why does AI sometimes make things up? Explained with a phone keyboard analogy.

Lesson 2/56 min readUpdated: November 2026

When you talk to Claude or ChatGPT, it feels like there's a person on the other side. In reality, you're talking to a very advanced prediction machine. Knowing this is the first step to getting better results from AI and catching its mistakes.

Language model: a giant “next word” predictor

At the core of Claude and ChatGPT is a program called a large language model, or LLM for short. The model is first trained on a huge amount of text: books, articles, code. In the first stage of training, it learns just one thing: what piece is most likely to come next in a text?

The phone keyboard analogy

When you type a message on your phone, the keyboard suggests the next word: type “See you” and “tomorrow” pops up. A language model is a much, much more advanced version of that. It doesn't produce one word; it produces paragraphs, tables and even working code. But at heart, what it does is still answer the question “what fits best next?”

A model that only predicts text doesn't make a good assistant. So in a second stage it's fine-tuned with human feedback: it's taught which answers are more helpful, more honest and safer. The assistant that follows instructions, asks questions and can say “I don't know” appears at this stage.

Tokens: AI's Lego bricks

The model doesn't read text letter by letter or word by word; it splits text into small pieces called tokens. A token is sometimes a whole word, sometimes part of a word, sometimes a single punctuation mark. In English, a token works out to a few characters on average.

The Lego analogy

Picture a Lego house. The house isn't one piece; it's made of small bricks. The model builds sentences out of token bricks the same way. A long word like “unbelievably” might be split into several pieces, roughly “un + believ + ably.” The actual split varies from model to model, and many languages other than English need more tokens for the same meaning.

Why do tokens matter? Because in AI tools, both usage limits and the amount of text a model can keep in mind at once are measured in tokens. The space the model can keep in front of it at one time is called the context window. Think of it as a kitchen counter: when it's full, you have to move old ingredients aside to make room for new ones. We'll cover this in detail in a lesson in Module 3.

What prediction means for you

  • The same question can get different answers. At each step the model picks one of the likely continuations. That's why two tries are never exactly the same. It's not a glitch; it's how it works.
  • Sounding fluent isn't the same as being right. The model can write smooth sentences even about things it isn't sure of. People call this “making things up”; the technical term is hallucination.
  • Its knowledge has an end date. The model is trained on text up to a certain date, called its knowledge cutoff. It can only know about something newer if it has a tool like web search and uses it.
  • It only knows what you tell it. Until you show it your business, your customers and your files, it guesses. The clearer the context you give, the more accurate the result.
Watch out

AI can get things wrong, especially numbers, dates, prices, legal rules and product specs. Don't use important information without checking it against an official source.

A 5-minute experiment

  1. Open a new chat in Claude or ChatGPT and ask: “Write 3 slogans for a small café.”
  2. Copy the answer and set it aside.
  3. Open a second new chat and type the exact same sentence.
  4. Compare the two answers. The fact that they differ shows the model predicts from scratch every time.
  5. Finally, send the prompt below and read how the model describes its own limits.
Tip

Telling the AI “If you're not sure, say so clearly” is a simple but effective habit that lowers the risk of made-up answers.

Key takeaways

  • A language model is a very advanced system that predicts how a text continues.
  • A token is a small piece the model splits text into; limits are measured in tokens.
  • A fluent answer isn't always a correct one; check important information.
